How we see color.

We learned in Section 4.1 that each of the senses has special transducers that convert the various forms of energy out in the world into electrical energy (in the form of action potentials), since that is the only type of energy that the brain "understands".

So, for example, transducers in the eye translate light energy into action potentials while transducers in the ear do the same thing with mechanical energy (in the form of sound waves). Transducers in the nose and on the tongue translate the chemical energy in odors and food into action potentials.

With regard to vision, there are two types of transducers. The first are rods, which are broadly responsible for translating aspects of light energy related to both movement and the basic distinction between light and dark. The second type of transducer in the eye is the cones, and they are in charge of translating the properties of light energy that allow us to see color.

There are three types of cones in the human eye — short wave (sometimes referred to as blue cones), medium wave (green cones), and long wave (red cones). Each of these cones responds differently to each specific wavelength of light. The pattern of activation across all three types of cones will determine the specific color that your brain will "see" during perception.

Figure 4.8 provides a simple representation of the relative activity in each of the three types of cones for several basic colors. You can see, for example, that short wave (blue) cones are the most active when we are perceiving the color blue (about 465 nm); medium (green) and long (red) wave cones are much less active when we are exposed to things that we perceive as blue.

Note for instructors: The cone curves are log-Gaussian approximations of the Stockman-Sharpe (2000) 2° cone fundamentals — peaks, widths, and the long-wavelength tail of the L-cone are reasonably accurate. L-cone activity at 750 nm is small but not zero (about 0.08%), and the readout shows two significant figures so this is visible. (On a linear-scale plot the tail still looks flat against the axis — that's expected; you'd need a log y-axis to see it as a curve.) A pedagogical aside: the "red" cone peaks in the yellow-orange (~565 nm), not the red, and the "green" cone peaks at yellow-green (~540 nm) — the colloquial names are misleading. The perceived color swatch uses a standard wavelength-to-RGB mapping; monitors can't reproduce pure spectral colors at the violet and red extremes, so those endpoints look dimmer than they would in real life.
