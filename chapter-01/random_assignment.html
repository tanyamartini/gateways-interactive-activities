Does it matter how we assign participants to groups in an experiment?

This activity focuses on some key concepts related to experimental research, which is described in Section 1.6.

One of the key advantages of true experiments is their ability to help us establish cause-and-effect relationships between variables.

Background to the Activity

There are a lot of stories in the news these days about the potential effects of young people's engagement with social media. In particular, several stories have reported on whether excessive use of social media can result in poorer academic performance. This seems like a reasonable concern, given that young people currently spend an average of 5 hours each day on social media sites such as Instagram and TikTok.

Imagine that Professor Anna Lyze is teaching a large psychology course and wants to run a study to test the hypothesis that reducing students' use of social media to 30 minutes per day for 7 days before an exam will cause an improvement in academic performance, as measured by their test scores on that exam.

Let's start with the basics: After reviewing Section 1.6, identify the independent and dependent variables in this experiment.

The independent variable (IV) is the amount of social media exposure (either limited to 30 minutes per day or unlimited). In a true experiment, we're examining potential cause-and-effect relationships between variables (reducing social media will cause an improvement in academic performance), and the independent measure is the variable that is assumed to be the "cause".

The dependent variable (DV) is academic performance. Remember that in a true experiment, we're examining potential cause-and-effect relationships between variables. The dependent measure is the "effect", and its name is appropriate because it's thought to "depend on" the independent variable.

For this study, Professor Anna Lyze will compare two groups. The experimental group ("limited social media" group) will agree to limit their engagement with social media to a maximum of 30 minutes per day for 7 days prior to the midterm exam. The control group ("unlimited social media" group) will not have any restrictions placed on their use of social media before the exam. After the grading is complete, the professor will compare the exam grades of students in the experimental and control groups.

Two hundred students from the class are recruited to take part in the study. How do we decide who goes in which group? The answer to that question matters more than you might think.

METHOD 01

Self-selection

Probably one of the simplest ways to allocate participants to groups would be to announce the study and assign the first 100 people who sign up to the experimental group — these are the people who would agree to reduce their social media usage to a maximum of 30 minutes per day in the 7 days leading up to the exam. The last 100 people to sign up become the control group. Researchers call this process of assigning people to groups self-selection, even though students aren't literally selecting their group. Instead, it's their behaviour — how quickly they sign up — that decides which group they end up in. In this way, participants' own actions are responsible for which group they're "selected" to be a part of. (Self-selection can also apply in other situations; for example, a study in which researchers were testing a program for older adults and put all of the people from one retirement home in the experimental group and all of the people from another retirement home in the control group.)

To see what happens in the study when we use self-selection to assign participants to groups, we're going to run a simulation of what the data would look like in this situation. Because this is a simulation, we have the advantage of controlling (and therefore knowing) something a real researcher never could: the true effect of limiting social media engagement on students' exam grades. Being able to control (and know) the true effect allows us to check the extent to which the results of self-selecting into groups would actually match reality. (In a real study, that true effect is the unknowable thing we're trying to establish.)

For this simulation of Dr. Lyze's study, we've set things up so that the true effect of reducing social media consumption is an increase of 4% on the midterm grade. Put another way, people who are in the "limited social media" (experimental) group will score 4% higher than people in the "unlimited social media" (control) group.

The simulation makes it clear that self-selection continually provided results that overestimated the size of the true effect (4%). In other words, the average exam mark for the experimental group always exceeded the average mark for the control group by much more than 4%. (It's worth noting that self-selection can also work in the opposite way; that is, in other studies, self-selection may continually underestimate the true effect.)

The take-home message is that self-selection just isn't a good way to assign participants to the experimental and control groups. But why not?

Let's think back to the self-selection process we used earlier to assign students to conditions. We assigned the first 100 people who signed up to the experimental (limited social media) condition, and the last 100 people to the control (unlimited social media) condition. Do you think that there might be a difference between the first 100 people who signed up for the study and the last 100 people?

This question is getting at the issue of extraneous variables, which are described in Section 1.6. Extraneous variables are variables other than the independent variable (social media exposure) that may have an effect on the dependent measure (grades).

Thinking about Dr. Anna Lyze's study, try to think of some variables other than daily time spent on social media (the independent variable) that might affect the students' midterm grades (the dependent variable).

Sometimes extraneous variables are associated with environmental conditions, but they can also reflect characteristics of the participants. For example, you could probably make a reasonable argument for the idea that academic motivation may be an extraneous variable — a variable other than the independent variable that could impact the dependent variable. More specifically, perhaps the first 100 people who signed up for the study were a bit more "keen", and academically motivated: When they saw a study aimed at potentially improving their grades, they jumped at the chance to take part.

METHOD 02

Matched groups

In Section 1.6, we talk about the fact that we often want to control for extraneous variables. To do this, we would need to measure them. In this case, we might have all participants complete a self-reported academic motivation scale — items like "I am driven to do well in my courses" and "I work hard at my studies."

If we did this, we could try to address some of the problems with self-selection by using a matched groups approach; that is, we put an equal number of high and low motivation people in both the experimental and control groups, so that the average level of motivation in each group is approximately equal. Put another way, we are going to "control for" motivation. Here, the idea is to make the two groups more comparable on the extraneous variable (academic motivation) before the study begins, since we suspect it matters for the outcome. That way, if we see a grade difference at the end, we can be more confident it's due to the independent variable (social media exposure), rather than to one group being more motivated than the other from the start.

METHOD 03

Random assignment

Because it's almost impossible to know all of the possible extraneous variables that might influence your results, the best way to assign people to groups is to use random assignment — flip a coin for each participant: heads you're in the experimental group, tails you're in the control group. Here, we're not relying on students' behavior or characteristics to decide who goes in which group; we're letting chance do the work. Any differences between the groups should be due to chance alone. More importantly, the laws of probability suggest that if you have a large number of participants and you use chance to determine assignment to groups, the difference in the average scores for the experimental and control groups should be very small for all extraneous variables (i.e., the difference in the average level of test anxiety or interest in the subject should be very small between the experimental and control groups).

Same study, same students, same question — but three different ways of assigning people to groups. Only random assignment, though, will provide you with the best chance of uncovering the true effect of the independent variable on the dependent variable. Why? Because random assignment provides the best alternative for ensuring that the two groups are as similar as possible across all of the potential extraneous variables that might impact the dependent measure. When that's the case, the only difference between the groups should be the independent variable (that is, whether or not they have limited their social media exposure). If we find a difference between the groups on the dependent measure (grades), then, we can be reasonably confident that it was the independent variable that caused it.
