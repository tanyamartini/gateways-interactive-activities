Exploring Relationships
Between Variables

Psychological researchers use different methods to study the relationships between variables. Three of the most common are experimental, quasi-experimental, and correlational methods. This activity walks you through all three — using the same research question each time — so you can see exactly what makes each design different.

Background to this Activity

Getting to Grips with Variables

We discuss variables in Section 1.6, defining them as any behavior, mental process, or characteristic that can change (or vary) across time, situations, or people. So in terms of behaviors and mental processes, people's frequency of depressive thinking is a variable, as are things like the amount of alcohol that people consume each day, our ability to remember things, feelings of loneliness or stress, levels of creativity or empathy, and behavioral expressions of things like sadism, leadership, or extroversion.

But as the definition states, personal details (that is, our characteristics) can also be variables. For example, marital status is a variable, as are religious and political affiliation, age, ethnicity, country of birth, and biological sex. But we're also measuring variables when we ask people about characteristics such as which football team they support, what type of food they most prefer, or their favorite social media platform.

Using these examples as a starting point, it's worth noting that there are two kinds of variables that you should be aware of: continuous variables and categorical (grouping) variables. Let's try to unpack the difference between them.

When we measure a variable, we're essentially assessing something about each person in our sample. For some variables, the answer when we assess that person will be a number that falls somewhere along a scale: How many hours did you sleep each night, on average, over the last six months? How often do you smoke cannabis? How comfortable do you feel in large groups? How happy do you feel when you engage with social media? Variables like these — where people's answers fall anywhere within a broad range along some kind of continuous scale — are called continuous variables.

For other variables, the answer to our questions will be a category label rather than a value on a scale: What is your nationality? Do you provide care for an elderly relative? What gender do you identify as? Which political party do you identify with? These are categorical variables. Rather than placing people's responses on a scale, a categorical variable sorts people into groups (which is why they are sometimes called grouping variables). So a categorical variable would be the basis for sorting people into parents vs. nonparents, Democrats vs. Republicans, or people whose favorite social media site is TikTok vs. Instagram vs. Facebook.

It's worth noting that continuous variables can be converted into categorical variables, but the reverse is not true. So, for example, if we were to ask a group of adults how old they were, this would be a continuous variable, and the answers might range along a scale from 18 to 100 years of age. However, we could use those (continuous) responses to divide people into three categorical groups such as younger (18–39 years), middle-aged (40–64 years), and older (65–100 years) adults.

To make sure you understand the difference between continuous and categorical variables, let's do a quick check on your understanding. One Question, Three Methods

The Role of Variables in Psychological Research

Most psychological research explores the relationships between variables. For example, does your level of social anxiety have any connection to the number of friends you have? Is attention span associated with grades in school? Is marital status related to happiness? Can cannabis consumption be linked to memory problems? Is biological sex connected to aggression?

Researchers can use different methods to address questions about the relationship between variables. Three that are very common are experimental methods (true experiments; see Section 1.6), quasi-experimental methods (Section 1.7), and correlational methods (Section 1.7). In this activity, we'll explore all three.

Each of the three methods outlined below examines the relationship between variables in different ways. For the purposes of this activity, we'll consider only the simplest possible case — studies involving the relationship between two variables only.

One important point to note before we begin: When figuring out what type of method a researcher is using, it's helpful to establish whether or not a categorical variable is being used to create groups of people for the purposes of comparison.

Method 1

Experimental

As we note in Section 1.6, experimental methods are special because they allow us to make causal statements about the relationship between two variables; for example, that stress causes anxiety to increase, or that exercise causes an increase in well-being, or that taking a particular drug causes a decrease in the symptoms of dementia.

Identifying whether a study is experimental requires that you look for two things. First, one variable will be categorical. The categorical (grouping) variable results in participants being put in one of two (or more) groups that will be compared. The second variable will typically be continuous.

Using the language of a true experiment, the categorical (grouping) variable is the independent variable, and the continuous variable is the dependent variable (see Section 1.6). After gathering data, the researcher will compare the average scores of the two groups (defined by the independent variable) on the dependent variable.

In Section 1.6 we note that the independent (grouping) variable is manipulated, or controlled, by the researcher. What that means is that the researcher is responsible for determining which group each participant is put in.

More important, though, is the fact that in a true experiment the researcher assigns participants to groups randomly — using, for example, the flip of a coin (heads the participant is in one group; tails they are in the other group), the roll of a die (even numbers means assignment to one group and odd numbers to the other group), or selecting a playing card from a deck (people who select red cards go in one group; black cards in the other).

Please see our interactive activity about random assignment to understand why it is so important to researchers' ability to make cause-and-effect statements about the relationship between the independent and dependent variables.

Let's take a look at an example.

The study: Researchers were interested in examining the relationship between sleep and problem-solving ability. These researchers recruited 800 university students for their study. They then used a coin flip to randomly assign each person to one of two groups. The "quality sleep group" received a structured plan that was known to help people achieve 8 hours of quality sleep per night. The other group (the "control" group) received no instructions about how to improve their sleep; they were just asked to engage in their normal sleep habits while taking part in the study. After one month, all of the participants were tested on their ability to solve a series of problems and each person was given a problem-solving score that ranged from 0–100%. Next, an average problem-solving score was calculated for the two groups and these average scores were compared.
1
Categorical and continuous variable. The categorical variable (independent variable) puts the participants in one of two groups (quality sleep group or control group). The continuous variable (dependent variable; problem-solving score) was measured for each participant; the average problem-solving score for each of the two groups is then compared after data are collected.
2
Random assignment on the categorical (independent) variable. Whether people were assigned to the quality sleep or control group was determined by chance (coin flip).
3
What we can conclude: If the quality sleep group has a better average problem-solving score than the control group at the end of the study, we have strong evidence that better sleep causes an improvement in problem solving.
Method 2

Quasi-Experimental

True experiments are special because they allow us to make causal statements about the relationship between two variables. The non-experimental research methods outlined in Section 1.7 also explore the relationships between variables, but they don't allow us to establish cause-and-effect relationships.

Quasi-experiments are one type of non-experimental method. They are, in many ways, very similar to true experiments. As is the case with experiments, one variable will be categorical (that is, a grouping variable) and the second variable will typically be continuous. Also similar to experiments, the researcher will compare the average scores of the groups on the continuous variable.

The key difference between true and quasi-experiments is that the researcher does not control (randomly assign) participants to the groups defined by the categorical variable, either because it would be impossible or unethical to do so.

For example, it's impossible to randomly assign people to groups when the categorical variable of interest is a fundamental characteristic of the person (for example, marital status, political affiliation, whether they are a parent or not). But sometimes we're interested in categorical variables for which random assignment would be deeply unethical. If we wanted to study the relationship between trauma (categorical variable: trauma survivor or not) and the quality of one's friendships, it would not be ethical for a researcher to randomly assign one half of their participants to a group that would experience trauma and the other half to a group that would not. The same would be true in a study designed to investigate whether smoking (categorical variable: smoker or not) was related to lung capacity — you would never get permission to randomly assign people to groups where they were forced to smoke (or not smoke). It's this inability to randomly assign participants to groups that prevents researchers from being able to make causal statements about the relationship between the variables.

The study: Imagine that the same group of researchers is still interested in the relationship between sleep and problem-solving ability. Unlike their true experiment, though, this time the researchers sorted 800 students into two groups according to the average number of hours of sleep these participants reported that they got each night. Participants who typically slept fewer than 8 hours per night were put in the "short sleepers" group and students who reported that they usually slept 8 or more hours per night were put in the "long sleepers" group. All of the participants completed the problem-solving test (achieving a score of 0–100%), and then the researchers compared the average problem-solving scores of the two groups. Note that in this situation, the researchers are not manipulating the independent variable (categorical variable: short or long sleepers) through random assignment — instead, participants were put into a group based on their existing sleep habits.
1
Categorical and continuous variable. The categorical variable (independent variable) puts the participants in one of two groups (short or long sleepers). The continuous variable (dependent variable; problem-solving score) was measured for each participant; the average problem-solving score for each of the two groups is then compared after data are collected.
2
No manipulation (random assignment) by researchers. Researchers don't control which group the participant is placed in. Instead, the group they are assigned to is determined by their own characteristics (the number of hours they reported sleeping each night).
3
What we can conclude: The amount of sleep that people get (short vs. long sleepers) appears to be related to problem-solving ability, but we cannot conclude that it causes changes in problem-solving ability. Why? Because short and long sleepers may also differ in many ways beyond how much they sleep — for example, work schedules, anxiety, family support, financial stress. Any of these other variables could explain the group difference in problem-solving scores, making it very hard to isolate sleep as the cause of observed differences between the groups.
Method 3

Correlational

The key to identifying a correlational study lies in the fact that we're typically examining a relationship between two continuous variables, rather than comparing groups. Here's an example, again examining the relationship between sleep and problem-solving ability:

The study: In this final study, the researchers surveyed another 800 university students. Each of these participants reported the average number of hours they slept the previous night (a continuous variable), and then the researchers administered the problem-solving test and obtained each person's score (0–100%; another continuous variable). In this study, no one was put into a group using a categorical variable. The researchers simply recorded the two numbers (number of hours slept and problem-solving score) for each participant and examined whether these two continuous variables were related.

Each dot represents one person in the sample. For clarity, only some of the data from the sample of 800 people is shown here.

1
Two continuous variables. For each participant, the researchers obtained a value for each of the two continuous variables — hours slept the previous night and problem-solving score. These values can be plotted on a scatterplot like the one above to give us a visual representation of the relationship between the two variables.
2
No groups. Because neither of the variables in this study is categorical, participants are not being sorted into groups.
3
What we can conclude: The fact that the points on the scatterplot appear to cluster around a line suggests that sleep and problem-solving ability are correlated — that is, there appears to be a relationship between them. Because the line moves up toward the right, this is a positive correlation (as one variable increases, the other also increases; see Section 1.7 for more about positive and negative correlations, as well as the use of correlation coefficients to establish the strength and direction of a correlation). Because this is a correlational study, we cannot say sleep causes better problem-solving (see Section 1.7 for more information about why correlational designs do not allow us to infer a causal relationship).
